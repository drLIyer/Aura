# Prospective Evidence on Migraine Trigger Effect Sizes (for Bayesian priors in a 24–48 h attack-risk model)

Method note: Research was done with web search only. Direct fetches of PubMed, PMC, Neurology, Springer and Taylor & Francis were blocked in this environment, so effect sizes come from abstracts, search-indexed snippets and secondary reports of the primary papers. Numbers marked "(secondary)" were relayed by a press or review source and not checked against the paper's tables. Quality grades: **High** = large prospective or case-crossover study, precise CI, replicated. **Moderate** = prospective within-person diary study with n of roughly 50–500 or a single study. **Low** = small n, not significant, ED/aggregate data, secondary report only, or not migraine-specific.

## Menstruation, estrogen withdrawal and ovulation

### Takeaway
Menstruation is the best-supported trigger in prospective diaries. Risk is about 1.7× in the 2 days before menses and about 2× on day 1 and the first days of bleeding. The mechanism is estrogen withdrawal in the late luteal and early follicular phase. I found no prospective effect size for ovulation.

### Cited Findings
- MacGregor & Hackshaw 2004, *Neurology* (prospective diary, n=155 women): compared with all other times of the cycle, migraine was **1.7 times more likely in the 2 days before menstruation** and 2.1 times more likely to be severe. The abstract gives no CI. — [PubMed 15277635](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15277635/)
- The AAN press release for the same data: +25% risk over the 5 premenstrual days, **+71% in the 2 days before menses**, and **more than double on day 1 of menstruation and within 5 days after onset**. Migraine with vomiting was almost 5× more likely on days 1–3. — [AAN press release](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/home/PressRelease/219); [ScienceDaily 2004](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/07/040727085303.htm)
- MacGregor et al. 2006, *Neurology* 67:2154–2158 (prospective, 3 cycles, daily urinary hormone assays plus diaries, 38 women with menstrual migraine): attacks were significantly more frequent than expected during the late luteal/early follicular phase of **falling estrogen** and less frequent during rising estrogen. I could not retrieve the observed/expected ratios and CIs. — [Neurology DOI 10.1212/01.wnl.0000233888.18228.19](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.wnl.0000233888.18228.19)
- The ICHD perimenstrual window is defined as day −2 to day +3 of menses. — [review description](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.wnl.0000310638.54698.36)
- Holsteen 2020, *Headache* (prospective diary, n=178, 1,870 migraine events): being **within 2 days of menstruation onset** was positively associated with next-day migraine risk in a multivariable model. The OR was not retrieved. — [PubMed 33022773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/); [Wiley DOI 10.1111/head.13960](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.13960)
- Park 2016, *PLoS One* (smartphone diary, 1,099 headache days): "hormonal changes" had **OR 3.5 (95% CI 2.3–5.2)**. Caveat: this OR compares migraine with non-migraine headache among headache days. It is not an attack-versus-no-attack risk ratio. — [PLoS One 10.1371/journal.pone.0149577](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)

### Inferences
- A perimenstrual RR of about 1.7–2.1 is reasonable for women with menstrually related migraine. In unselected female migraineurs the population average is probably lower, because many women have no menstrual association. MacGregor's samples were selected for menstrual attacks.
- The prior should peak at days −2 to +3 and apply only to naturally cycling users. Hormonal contraception changes the pattern, and Mostofsky's cohort found effect modification by OC use for caffeine.

### Gaps
- I found no prospective RR or CI for **ovulation** (mid-cycle estrogen fall).
- The exact CIs for MacGregor 2004/2006 and the PAMINA (Wöber 2007, *Cephalalgia* 27:304–14, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01279.x) menstruation hazard ratio could not be retrieved.

## Stress, stress let-down and negative emotions

### Takeaway
The best-supported stress exposure is a **decline** in stress (let-down), with OR about 1.5–1.9 over the next 6–18 h. Absolute stress level showed a weak or inconsistent association in Lipton 2014. Larger diary cohorts (Houle, Holsteen) find a modest positive effect of higher stress.

### Cited Findings
- Lipton et al. 2014, *Neurology* 82:1395–1401 (prospective electronic diary, 17 completers out of 22 enrolled, 2,011 diary entries, 110 attacks): a decline in Perceived Stress Scale score from one evening to the next was associated with migraine onset in the following 6, 12 and 18 h, with **ORs ranging from 1.5 to 1.9 (all p<0.05)**. Stress **level** was generally not associated. Press materials cite a "nearly five-fold" risk in the first 6 h, which does not match the abstract and should not be used. — [CDC Stacks abstract](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/200156); [Neurology DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000000332](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000000332); [Montefiore press](https://www.montefiore.org/migraine-attacks-increase-following-stress-let-down)
- Houle et al. 2017, *Headache* (prospective diary, n=95, 4,195–4,626 diary days, headache on 38.5% of days): a forecasting model based on the frequency or intensity of stressful events reached **AUC 0.73 (0.71–0.75) in training and 0.65 (0.60–0.67) in leave-one-out validation**. — [PubMed 28699328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28699328/)
- Holsteen 2020: **higher stress** was positively associated with next-day migraine risk in the multivariable model. — [PubMed 33022773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/)
- Park 2016 (smartphone diary): stress **OR 1.8** for migraine versus non-migraine headache. Stress was the most common reported trigger, present for 57.7% of triggered headaches. — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)
- Lateef et al. 2024, *Neurology* (community EMA, n=477, 4×/day for 2 weeks, 4,974 assessments): higher average stress and **rising energy** on the prior day predicted pm headaches (β=0.157, 0.032–0.281). **Falling energy** (β=−0.145) and lower or decreasing sleep quality predicted am headaches. Mood and anxiety were not significant after adjustment. Results are reported as β coefficients, not ORs. — [Neurology DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000208102](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000208102); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20240201/sleep-quality-energy-mood-stress-associated-with-incident-diurnal-headache-time-onsetin-older-adults)

### Inferences
- Model stress as two features: (a) a level effect with RR about 1.1–1.2 per high-stress day, and (b) a let-down effect with RR about 1.4–1.5 for a day-over-day decline. The Lipton estimate comes from only 17 people and should be shrunk.
- Some of the stress and "low energy" signal may be prodrome (reverse causation), as Lipton's authors note.

### Gaps
- I found no prospective effect sizes specific to **anger, arguments or conflict**.
- The exact Lipton OR/CI for each time window was not retrieved. The abstract gives only a range of 1.5–1.9.

## Sleep: short sleep, poor quality, fragmentation, oversleeping

### Takeaway
Prospective data do **not** support short sleep duration as a next-day trigger. Low self-reported sleep efficiency and fragmentation show a modest effect (OR about 1.4 on day +1). Actigraphy results are inconsistent.

### Cited Findings
- Bertisch et al. 2020, *Neurology* (prospective cohort, n=98 episodic migraine, 6 weeks of twice-daily diaries plus wrist actigraphy): **low diary sleep efficiency → OR 1.39 (95% CI 1.06–1.81)** for headache on the next day (secondary report). **Short sleep duration and poor sleep quality were not associated** with migraine on day 0 or day 1. Actigraphic fragmentation was paradoxically associated with **lower** same-day odds: WASO >53 min OR 0.64 (0.48–0.86), efficiency ≤88% OR 0.74 (0.56–0.99). Sleep >8.5 h was associated with **lower** risk 2 days later, which argues against oversleeping as a trigger. — [Neurology DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000008740](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000008740); [MDedge/Hospitalist summary](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/how-does-sleep-affect-migraine-risk); [Brigham press release](https://www.brighamandwomens.org/about-bwh/newsroom/press-releases-detail?id=3522)
- Lateef 2024 (community EMA, n=477): lower average sleep quality and a decrease in sleep quality predicted next-morning headache (β=−0.172, −0.305 to −0.039). — [Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000208102)
- Park 2016: "sleep deprivation" was reported as the trigger for 55.1% of triggered headaches. This is a self-attribution frequency, not a risk ratio. — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)
- Mosek & Korczyn 1995: oversleeping did not explain fasting headache. — [Neurology 10.1212/WNL.45.11.1953](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.45.11.1953)

### Inferences
- Use a modest prior of RR about 1.3 for poor or fragmented self-reported sleep the previous night, and near-null (RR about 1.05) for short duration alone.
- I found no evidence that oversleeping is a trigger. A weak null prior is appropriate.
- Tiredness and poor sleep may partly be prodrome (yawning and fatigue are classic premonitory symptoms).

### Gaps
- The day-1 actigraphy OR was not retrieved. Some press summaries conflict on the direction of the actigraphy findings.
- I found no prospective cumulative "sleep debt" effect size.

## Fasting, skipped meals and hypoglycemia

### Takeaway
Fasting reliably increases headache. The data come from natural experiments (Yom Kippur, Ramadan), not day-level diary ORs, and the outcomes are mostly "headache" or migraine-day counts.

### Cited Findings
- Mosek & Korczyn 1995, *Neurology* (prospective before/after cohort, n=370; 211 fasted 25 h on Yom Kippur): headache in **39% of fasters vs 7% of non-fasters** (crude risk ratio about 5.6). The figures were 66% vs 29% in those with versus without a headache history. Risk rose with fast duration. Caffeine withdrawal did not explain it. Most of these headaches were non-pulsating tension-type, not migraine. — [Neurology 10.1212/WNL.45.11.1953](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.45.11.1953); [PubMed 7501139](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7501139/)
- Mosek & Korczyn 1999, *Headache* (n=56): acute weight loss and dehydration did not explain fasting headache. Fifty percent of participants had headache. — [TAU record](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/fasting-headache-weight-loss-and-dehydration/)
- Abu-Salameh et al. 2010, *J Headache Pain* (cohort crossover, n=32 migraineurs, 30 analyzed): **9.4±4.3 migraine days during Ramadan vs 3.7±2.1 in a control month** (p<0.001), about a 2.5× rate ratio. — [Springer 10.1007/s10194-010-0242-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-010-0242-z)
- Prospective Egyptian diary study (n=292): attack frequency was higher in Ramadan (median 4, IQR 2–7) than in Shaaban (median 3, IQR 1–6; p=0.009), about 1.3×. Frequency declined over the course of the month. — [PubMed 34726767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34726767/)
- Park 2016: overeating had OR 2.4 for migraine versus non-migraine headache. — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)

### Inferences
- Total fasting probably carries an RR of 1.3–2.5 for migraine days. A single skipped or late meal is likely weaker. A conservative prior is RR about 1.3 for a skipped meal and about 1.6 for a fast of 16 h or more.
- Ramadan studies are confounded by sleep shifts and caffeine changes.

### Gaps
- I found no within-person diary OR for skipped or late meals or for measured hypoglycemia.

## Alcohol, red wine and caffeine (intake and withdrawal)

### Takeaway
In prospective cohorts, ordinary alcohol intake does **not** raise next-day risk. Only heavy intake (5 or more drinks) shows a signal. Caffeine has a J-shaped, threshold-like pattern: the 1–2 servings estimate falls below 1 (not significant), and the per-level estimates for 3 or more servings are elevated but individually non-significant. A **decrease** in caffeine predicted risk in Holsteen, which is consistent with withdrawal.

### Cited Findings
- Vives-Mestres et al. 2022, *Headache* (prospective app cohort, N1-Headache, n=487 episodic migraine): alcohol on day −1 **OR 1.01 (0.91–1.11)**. Alcohol on day −2 **OR 0.75 (0.68–0.82)**, i.e. lower risk. Each additional drink 2 days earlier was associated with a 12% lower probability of attack. — [Headache 10.1111/head.14428](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.14428); [MedicalXpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2022-12-alcohol-migraine-hours.html)
- Mostofsky et al. 2020, *Annals of Medicine* (prospective cohort, n=98, 6 weeks): 1–2 alcohol servings were not associated with next-day headache. **5 or more servings: OR 2.08 (1.16–3.73)** for next-day headache. The outcome was headache, not migraine-specific. — [Tandfonline 10.1080/07853890.2020.1758340](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07853890.2020.1758340)
- Park 2016: alcohol OR 2.5 for migraine versus non-migraine headache (headache-day comparison, not attack risk). — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)
- Mostofsky et al. 2019, *Am J Med* (prospective case-crossover diary, n=98, 4,467 days, 825 migraines): there was a significant **nonlinear association (p-quadratic=0.024)**, but no single intake level was significant. Same-day ORs: **1–2 servings OR 0.84 (0.63–1.13)**, **4 servings OR 1.41 (0.66–3.0)**, **5+ servings OR 2.61 (0.97–7.05)** (secondary report). Press reports describe higher odds at 3 or more servings, and one report gives OR about 1.4 without a CI. The effect was strongest in low habitual users (<1 serving/day) and attenuated in OC users. — [Am J Med 10.1016/j.amjmed.2019.02.015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002934319302104); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/how-many-caffeine-servings-trigger-migraine-headaches); [caffeine systematic review 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185183/)
- Mittleman/Mostofsky 2024, *Headache* (same cohort): **habitual** intake did not affect headache frequency (7.1 vs 7.4 vs 5.9 days/month for 0, 1–2 and 3–4 servings/day). — [PMC10954400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10954400/)
- Holsteen 2020: a **decrease in caffeine consumption** was positively associated with next-day migraine risk. This supports caffeine withdrawal as a trigger; the OR was not retrieved. — [PubMed 33022773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/)

### Inferences
- Use an alcohol prior near RR 1.0 for 1–4 drinks and about 1.3–1.5 for 5 or more drinks the night before. Shrink the 2.08 estimate toward the null because the outcome was headache and n was small.
- Model caffeine relative to the person's habitual baseline: 3 or more servings above usual gives RR about 1.2–1.3, and a drop below usual gives RR about 1.2.

### Gaps
- I found no prospective effect size specific to **red wine**. The Onderwater 2019 study is a cross-sectional survey.
- The Mostofsky 2019 ORs for exactly 3 servings and the next-day ORs were not retrieved.

## Weather: temperature, barometric pressure and humidity

### Takeaway
Population-level weather effects are small: about 5–8% per 5°C or 5 mmHg. Prospective diaries show that only a subgroup of patients is weather-sensitive, and individual-level prediction from weather is poor.

### Cited Findings
- Mukamal et al. 2009, *Neurology* (case-crossover, n=7,054 ED headache visits, Boston): a **5°C higher temperature over the prior 24 h gave OR 1.075 (1.021–1.133)**. Lower barometric pressure 48–72 h before raised the risk of **non-migraine** headache (OR 0.939 per 5 mmHg higher pressure, 0.902–0.978, i.e. about 1.065 per 5 mmHg drop). Air pollution had no effect. — [PubMed 19273827](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19273827/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/03/090309161951.htm)
- Li et al. 2019, *Environment International* 132:105100 (prospective, n=98 episodic migraine, about 45 days of follow-up): higher **relative humidity** was associated with migraine onset in the warm season only. Associations weakened and were not significant in the overall GEE analysis. In the cold season there were associations with ozone and CO. ORs were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412019300662); [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/study-examines-links-between-weather-pollution-migraines)
- Hoffmann et al. 2015, *Ann Clin Transl Neurol* 2:22–28 (prospective diary, n=100, 12 months): **no significant association** in the pooled analysis. Only a subgroup was weather-sensitive, and individual prediction from meteorological data was not possible. — [Wiley 10.1002/acn3.139](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acn3.139)
- Kimoto et al. 2011, *Intern Med* (prospective diary, n=28): weather change was associated with migraine in 18 of 28 patients, and 14 named low pressure. No RR is available. — [PubMed 21921370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21921370/)
- Zebenholzer et al. 2011, *Cephalalgia* (PAMINA weather analysis, prospective diary): weather was examined as a modest influence. Effect sizes were not retrieved. — [SAGE 10.1177/0333102410385580](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0333102410385580)
- Yang et al. 2015, *J Headache Pain* (1-year diary, n=66, Taipei): temperature change explained 29.2% of the variance in winter headache incidence in self-reported temperature-sensitive patients, and was not significant in the others. No RR was reported. — [PMC4446287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4446287/)
- Elcik et al. 2017, *Int J Biometeorol* 61:2245–54 (ED visits by air mass type): migraine ED visits were higher during tropical air masses. The RR was not retrieved. — [DOI 10.1007/s00484-017-1432-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00484-017-1432-z)
- A 2025 meta-analysis reportedly gives a pooled temperature **OR 1.15 (1.02–1.29)**, pressure **OR 1.07 (1.01–1.15)** and humidity **OR 1.04 (0.97–1.11, not significant)**. **This is unverified**: it was relayed by a search summary of a Springer *J Neurol* paper that I could not open. — [J Neurol 10.1007/s00415-025-13078-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13078-0)

### Inferences
- Use a population prior of RR about 1.05 per 5°C rise and about 1.05 per 5 hPa drop over 24–48 h. Allow a heavy-tailed individual random effect so that the minority of weather-sensitive patients can learn larger effects.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a pressure-drop threshold from primary sources. A ">5 hPa" figure attributed to Kimoto appears only on commercial sites.

## Physical exertion, dehydration, screen time and long working hours

### Takeaway
Prospective and provocation evidence for exertion as a trigger is weak. For dehydration, screen time and long working hours I found no prospective effect sizes.

### Cited Findings
- Hougaard et al. 2013, *Neurology* (provocation, n=27 patients with migraine with aura who reported light or exercise triggers): only **3 of 27 (11%) developed migraine with aura** and 3 more had migraine without aura. **4 of 12** had migraine after exercise and **0** after photostimulation. — [PMC3620071](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3620071/); [Neurology 10.1212/WNL.0b013e31827f0f10](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e31827f0f10)
- Mosek & Korczyn 1999: dehydration measured as weight loss did not explain fasting headache. — [TAU record](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/fasting-headache-weight-loss-and-dehydration/)
- Park 2016: traveling had OR 6.4 (1.2–10.2, very imprecise) and noise OR 2.8 for migraine versus non-migraine headache days. — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)

### Inferences
- Use near-null priors (RR 1.0–1.1) for exertion, dehydration, screen time and work hours, and let individual data move them.

### Gaps
- I found no prospective diary ORs for screen time, long working hours or measured hydration.

## Premonitory symptoms and reverse causality

### Takeaway
Premonitory symptoms (yawning, fatigue, photophobia, neck stiffness, food craving) often precede attacks by hours. Many apparent "triggers" (chocolate craving, light sensitivity, tiredness, perceived poor sleep) are likely early attack phase rather than causes. Their predictive value is real but time-dependent and modest.

### Cited Findings
- Giffin et al. 2003, *Neurology* (prospective electronic diary, n=97 analyzed of about 120): when premonitory symptoms were recorded, **72% were followed by migraine**. Prediction was better when symptoms appeared about 6 h before onset (more than two-thirds) than about 18 h before (less than one-fifth). — [PubMed 12654956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12654956/); [PMC6166762 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6166762/)
- A prospective study reviewed in Pavlovic/others reported a PPV of 85.1% for a recorded premonitory symptom (attack within 72 h). Photophobia, fatigue, sadness, drowsiness and yawning were the most accurate. A later EMA study found PPV of only about 20%. — [PMC6166762](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6166762/); [PMC13337128](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13337128/)
- Hougaard 2013: self-perceived light triggers did not reproduce attacks (0 of patients after photostimulation). This is consistent with photophobia being prodromal rather than causal. — [PMC3620071](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3620071/)
- Peris et al. 2017, *Cephalalgia* 37:452–463 (90-day diaries, n=326, N-of-1 analyses of 33 factors): potential triggers or premonitory factors were identified in 87% of patients (about 4 each), and profiles were unique in 85%. Only 8 of 33 factors were associated at the population level. — [PubMed 27179352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27179352/)
- PAMINA 2016 reliability analysis: retrospective questionnaires over- or under-estimate triggers compared with prospective diaries. — [Eur J Neurol 10.1111/ene.12817](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ene.12817)

### Inferences
- In a forecasting model, premonitory symptoms should be **predictor features, not triggers**, with large short-horizon weight (≤12 h) and little weight at 24–48 h. Prodromal features (craving, light sensitivity, yawning, fatigue, neck pain, low energy) should be excluded from "trigger" causal priors.

### Gaps
- The exact Giffin 2003 diagnostic accuracy statistics (sensitivity) and the Karsan/Schulte prospective PPV data were not retrieved.

## Forecasting and prediction models (accuracy)

### Takeaway
Within-person discrimination from diary triggers is modest: AUC of about 0.56–0.65 for validated or within-person models. One large commercial app study claims AUC 0.89, but it is industry-linked. Wearable-based models are still small proof-of-concept studies.

### Cited Findings
- Houle 2017 (stress model, n=95): **AUC 0.65 (0.60–0.67)** in leave-one-out validation and 0.73 in training. — [PubMed 28699328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28699328/)
- Holsteen 2020 (multivariable daily-trigger model, n=178, 1,870 events): **within-person C-statistic 0.56 (0.54–0.58)**. Predictors were caffeine decrease, self-predicted headache probability, stress and days 0–2 of menstruation. — [PubMed 33022773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/); [Buse 2020 editorial](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.14025)
- Stubberud et al. 2023, *Cephalalgia* (diary plus wearable skin temperature, HRV and EMG, about 18 patients, 295 days): random forest **AUC 0.62** on hold-out data. — [PubMed 37096352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37096352/); [SAGE 10.1177/03331024231169244](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03331024231169244)
- Rabany/Cowan et al. 2026, *Neurology Open Access* (Nerivio app, n=53,065, 770,473 report days, XGBoost on diary, questionnaires and weather): **AUC 0.893**, precision 0.912, sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.83. The work is company-affiliated, and inflation from autocorrelation or recent-attack features is possible. — [Neurology OA 10.1212/WN9.0000000000000144](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WN9.0000000000000144); [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/theranicas-ai-achieves-91-precision-on-next-day-migraine-likelihood-in-largest-study-of-its-kind-302887129.html)
- Wrist-sensor sleep data (n=7): balanced accuracy above 84% for detecting an attack one night ahead, with large between-subject variation. — [PMC5981434](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5981434/)
- An HRV-in-sleep ML study (n=23, 2026) exists, but its accuracy was not retrieved. — [PubMed 41607086](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41607086/)

### Inferences
- A realistic target for a personal Bayesian model is a within-person AUC of about 0.60–0.70. Claims of AUC above 0.85 likely depend on persistence or recent-attack features rather than triggers.

### Gaps
- I found no robust prospective effect sizes for resting heart rate change before attacks.
