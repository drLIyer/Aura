# Self-reported (perceived) prevalence of migraine triggers worldwide

> Method note: PubMed, PMC, Europe PMC, SAGE, Springer and Frontiers full-text pages could not be fetched from this environment (DNS and proxy blocks). All figures below come from abstracts and indexed excerpts returned by web search. Each figure is cited to its primary article (DOI or PubMed). Where a figure could only be partly checked, it is marked **[partly verified]**. Every number in these notes is a **perceived / self-reported** prevalence (the share of patients who say a factor triggers attacks), unless it is labelled "prospective".

## Q1. What percentage of people with migraine report each trigger?

### Takeaway
About 75–90% of clinic patients with migraine report at least one trigger. Stress is the trigger reported most often almost everywhere (about 58% pooled; 64–80% in clinic series). Next come sleep disturbance (about 41% pooled; 44–64% in clinics), menstruation/hormones in women (about 65%), fasting or skipped meals (about 46–57%) and weather (about 50%). Sensory triggers (odours, light), alcohol and specific foods sit in the middle range (about 25–45%). Exercise (about 13–22%), caffeine (about 8%) and sexual activity (about 2–5%) are reported least.

### Cited Findings
**Kelman 2007, Cephalalgia 27(5):394–402, US tertiary headache clinic, retrospective, n=1,207 ICHD-2 migraine patients** — [DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x); [PubMed 17403039](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17403039/)
- 75.9% reported any trigger: 40.4% infrequently, 26.7% frequently and 8.8% very frequently (0–3 scale).
- Stress 79.7%
- Hormones (women only) 65.1%
- Not eating / missed meal / hunger / fasting 57.3%
- Weather 53.2%
- Sleep disturbance 49.8%
- Perfume / odour 43.7%
- Neck pain 38.4% (Marmura 2018 notes this may be a premonitory symptom rather than a trigger)
- Lights 38.1%
- Alcohol 37.8%
- Smoke 35.7%
- Food 26.9%
- Exercise 22.1%
- Sexual activity 5.2% (least cited)
- [Partly verified] The abstract also lists "sleeping late" (about 32.0%) and "heat" (about 30.3%). These come from memory of the abstract and were not confirmed in this session's search results. Check them before use.
- Of the 1,750 migraine patients seen at first visit, 84.3% were female. Triggers were linked to more florid acute attacks. — same source
- The abstract reports differences between women and men, with vs without aura, episodic vs chronic, and migraine vs probable migraine. The direction of each difference was not retrievable (see Gaps). — same source

**Pellegrino et al. 2018, Cephalalgia, meta-analysis of perceived triggers in primary headache (migraine + TTH)** — [DOI 10.1177/0333102417727535](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102417727535); [PubMed 28825314](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28825314/)
- 85 articles (1958–2015), 27,122 participants, 420 unique triggers grouped into 15 categories.
- 81% (95% CI 75–86%) endorsed at least one trigger. This pooled figure covers migraine and TTH together. A migraine-only figure of about 76% circulates in secondary sources but was not confirmed as the paper's pooled estimate.
- Stress: pooled 0.58 (95% CI 0.53–0.63)
- Sleep: 0.41 (0.36–0.47)
- Medications were the least-endorsed category.
- Endorsement rose with the number of trigger categories asked about (OR 1.18, 1.08–1.30 per category) and with publication year (OR 1.04, 1.00–1.08). The authors describe "extreme heterogeneity", and say endorsement "is influenced by method of assessment".
- The pooled rates for the other 13 categories (hormones, fasting, weather, alcohol, food, light, noise, odours, heat, exercise, smoking and so on) were not retrievable.

**Andress-Rothrock, King & Rothrock 2010, Headache 50(8):1366–70, US clinic, 200 consecutive new migraine patients** — [DOI 10.1111/j.1526-4610.2010.01753.x](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1526-4610.2010.01753.x); [PubMed 21044280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21044280/)
- 91% (182/200) reported at least 1 trigger.
- 82.5% (165/200) reported multiple triggers.
- Caffeine was reported by about 8%. This figure comes from a citing paper and is **[partly verified]**.

**Mollaoğlu 2013, J Health Psychol 18(7), Turkey, n=126 migraine patients** — [DOI 10.1177/1359105312446773](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1359105312446773)
- Emotional stress 79%
- Sleep disturbance 64%
- Dietary factors 44%

**Fukui et al. 2008, Arq Neuropsiquiatr 66(3A):494–9, Brazil, 200 consecutive migraine patients** — [SciELO, DOI 10.1590/S0004-282X2008000400011](https://www.scielo.br/j/anp/a/8dGbJR6MJRfR9pvdxNBgFVs/abstract/?lang=en); [PubMed 18813707](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18813707)
- Emotional stress 64%
- Physical activity 13%
- Sexual activity 2.5%
- An Academia.edu copy lists sleep problems at 81% **[partly verified; the attribution is uncertain]**.

**Hauge, Kirchmann & Olesen 2011, Cephalalgia, Denmark, n=181 patients with migraine with aura (MA)** — [DOI 10.1177/0333102410382795](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102410382795); [PubMed 20847084](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20847084/)
- Share of patients reporting that a factor triggers at least 50% of their attacks:
  - Hormones 62%
  - Light 47%
  - Stress 42%
  - Alcohol 0%
- Specific sub-triggers among patients who reported the parent category:
  - Too much work 54/64
  - Reflected sunlight 35/44
  - Too little sleep 19/24
  - Red wine 20/22
  - Passive smoking 11/11
  - Menstruation 12/14
  - Perfume 12/15

**Weather, meta-analytic:** the pooled share reporting weather change as a trigger had a risk difference of 0.47 (95% CI 0.40–0.54). — [J Neurol 2025, weather and migraine SR/MA](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13078-0)

**Peris et al. 2017, Cephalalgia 37(5):452–63, n=326 patients, 90-day diaries on 33 factors (prospective N-of-1 analysis, not self-report prevalence)** — [PubMed 27179352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27179352/)
- Individual factor–attack profiles could be built for 87% of patients.
- Patients had a mean of 4 associated factors each.
- 85% of profiles were unique.
- Every one of the 33 factors was associated with attacks in some patients, but only 8 were significant in the pooled analysis.

**Marmura 2018, Curr Pain Headache Rep, review "Triggers, Protectors, and Predictors in Episodic Migraine"** — [DOI 10.1007/s11916-018-0734-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11916-018-0734-0)
- The most commonly mentioned triggers are stress, the menstrual cycle, weather changes, sleep disturbance, alcohol and other foods.
- Clinical studies have verified fasting, the premenstrual period, "let-down" after stress and probably low barometric pressure as triggers.
- Premonitory symptoms such as neck pain, fatigue and sensitivity to light, sound or odour can mimic triggers. No percentages were retrieved.

**Premonitory confounding:** some perceived triggers (light, sound, foods, skipped meals) may be early premonitory manifestations of the attack. — [J Neurol 2021, DOI 10.1007/s00415-020-10344-1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-020-10344-1)

**Prospective cross-reference (not the focus of these notes):** in a Korean smartphone-diary study of episodic migraine, the share of headache days with each trigger was stress 27.6%, fatigue 20.7%, sleep deprivation 20.4%, hormonal change 11.5% and weather 9.9%. The probability of headache given an exposure was stress 57.7%, sleep deprivation 55.1%, fatigue 48.5% and any trigger 46.5%. — [Park et al., PLoS One 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0149577](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0149577)

### Inferences
- Suggested starting priors for "proportion of patients who report trigger X" in a clinic population:
  - Stress: about 0.6–0.8
  - Menstruation/hormones (women): about 0.6
  - Fasting/skipped meals: about 0.45–0.55
  - Sleep (too little or disrupted): about 0.4–0.6
  - Weather: about 0.45–0.5
  - Odours: about 0.4
  - Light: about 0.4
  - Alcohol: about 0.35
  - Smoke: about 0.35
  - Heat: about 0.3
  - Food: about 0.25
  - Exercise: about 0.15–0.2
  - Caffeine: about 0.1
  - Sex: about 0.05
- These are endorsement rates, not attack-level risks. Self-report inflates them: they rise with longer checklists and with clinic samples. Neck pain, light, noise and odour may be premonitory rather than causal. A model should down-weight these relative to fasting, menstruation, sleep and stress, which have some prospective support (Marmura 2018).
- The prior should allow for idiosyncrasy. Peris 2017 found that only about 4 factors per patient were genuinely associated with attacks, and that profiles are mostly unique.

### Gaps
- Pellegrino 2018 category-level pooled rates other than stress and sleep (hormones, fasting, weather, food, alcohol, light, noise, odours, heat, exercise, smoking) could not be retrieved, because full text was inaccessible.
- No reliable survey percentages were found for dehydration, screen use, travel (beyond India, below), emotional conflict/arguments as a separate item, or overwork (beyond Hauge 2011 and Ethiopia, below).
- Kelman's "sleeping late" and "heat" figures need verification against the abstract.

## Q2. How do reported triggers differ by region?

### Takeaway
Stress and sleep rank at the top in every region. Regional differences sit in the second tier: fasting (Ramadan) is prominent in West Asia and the Middle East, weather and barometric pressure in East Asia (especially Japan), and sun or heat exposure in South Asia, Africa and China. Alcohol is reported less in Muslim-majority regions. The comparative data are thin and methodologically heterogeneous.

### Cited Findings
**Asia overview: Iba et al. 2023, Front Neurol, narrative review of 42 papers from 13 Asian countries (2000–Feb 2022)** — [DOI 10.3389/fneur.2023.1169795](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1169795/full); [PubMed 37206912](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37206912/)
- Stress and sleep are the most frequently reported triggers across Asia.
- Fatigue and weather are common in East Asia; fasting is common in West Asia.
- Food and alcohol triggers are shaped by culture.
- Weather triggers are highly heterogeneous between regions.

**Global overview: Ihara, García-Azorín, Iba … Pozo-Rosich, Takizawa 2026, Curr Pain Headache Rep 30:128, "A Global Perspective on Migraine Attack Triggers", experts from 11 countries** — [DOI 10.1007/s11916-026-01547-8](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/a-global-perspective-on-migraine-attack-triggers-understanding-di/)
- Stress, menstruation and sleep disturbance are the most reported triggers worldwide.
- Culture-specific triggers include fasting during Ramadan or Yom Kippur.
- Region-specific triggers include barometric pressure (East Asia), Lodos winds (Türkiye), the midnight sun (Arctic) and watermelon (Brazil).
- No percentages were retrieved.

A further review exists: "Geographical Differences in Trigger Factors of Tension-Type Headaches and Migraines", Curr Pain Headache Rep 2019. — [DOI 10.1007/s11916-019-0760-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11916-019-0760-6) (content not retrieved)

**North America**
- Kelman 2007 (USA, n=1,207) and Andress-Rothrock 2010 (USA, n=200): see Q1.

**Europe**
- **Netherlands, van Casteren et al. 2021, Cephalalgia 41(6):643–8, n=5,725 women + 1,061 men:** see Q3. — [DOI 10.1177/0333102420974362](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102420974362)
- **Denmark, Hauge 2011:** see Q1.
- **Greece, Iliopoulos et al. 2015, BMC Res Notes 8:393, n=116 tertiary patients, 35-item questionnaire:**
  - Stressful life events, then intense emotions, were the most frequent triggers in all subgroups.
  - Percentages were not retrieved.
  - — [DOI 10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7)
- **Greece, Constantinides et al. 2015, Arq Neuropsiquiatr, n=51 migraine + 12 TTH:**
  - Dietary triggers were associated with migraine vs TTH.
  - Stress was common in both groups.
  - — [PubMed 26222357](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26222357/)

**Latin America**
- **Brazil, Fukui 2008 (n=200):**
  - Stress 64%
  - Physical activity 13%
  - Sexual activity 2.5%
  - — [SciELO](https://www.scielo.br/j/anp/a/8dGbJR6MJRfR9pvdxNBgFVs/abstract/?lang=en)
- **Brazilian adolescents:** a study reports mainly environmental triggers. — [Arq Neuropsiquiatr](https://scielo.br/j/anp/a/rKs9nhmCRbxKSXK5NkKGfJP/?lang=en) (percentages not retrieved)
- No multi-country Latin American trigger survey was found. Ihara 2026 cites watermelon as a Brazil-specific trigger.

**East Asia**
- **China, Chongqing tertiary clinic, n=1,219 headache patients (394 migraine):**
  - 80.2% of migraineurs reported triggers.
  - The top triggers were sleep disturbance, negative affect and sunlight.
  - Compared with TTH, migraine was more associated with weather change (OR 3.1, 95% CI 1.9–4.8), menstruation, noise and alcohol.
  - — [PubMed 23356519](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356519/) (per-trigger percentages not retrieved)
- **Western China medical students with migraine:**
  - Staying up late 57.0%
  - Stress 55.3%
  - Cold 54.3%
  - Sleep problems 52.3%
  - — [PMC9125914](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125914)
- **Japan:**
  - Weather change was reported by 39.2% of men vs 56.1% of women; fatigue/malaise by 47.9% vs 51.9%.
  - Women reported environmental triggers (weather, light, noise) more often; men reported lifestyle triggers more often.
  - — [PMC13403815, "Clinical and social burden of migraine in Japanese males"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13403815/)
  - A 2025 Japan-focused review says the weather effect in Japan "remains unclear". — [Front Neurol 2025, 10.3389/fneur.2025.1600822](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2025.1600822/full)
- **Korea:**
  - Stress, then sleep deprivation, then fatigue are the leading triggers in hospital data.
  - Diary data are given in Q1 (Park 2016).
- **Taiwan:** no trigger-prevalence data were found.

**South Asia**
- **India, Yadav et al. 2010, Pain Med, n=182 migraine patients:**
  - 87.9% (160/182) reported triggers.
  - Emotional stress 70%
  - Physical exhaustion/travel 52.5%
  - Fasting 46.3%
  - Sleep deprivation 44.4%
  - Menstruation was about 12.8% of the trigger-positive group **[partly verified]**.
  - — [DOI 10.1111/j.1526-4637.2009.00725.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1526-4637.2009.00725.x)
- **Eastern India population survey (Neurology India):** the leading triggers were prolonged sun exposure, then sleep deprivation, then long-distance travel (percentages not retrieved). — [Neurol India, 10.4103/0028-3886.217979](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/neur/fulltext/10.4103/0028-3886.217979~prevalence-burden-and-risk-factors-of-migraine-a)
- **India, children at a public hospital:** — [PubMed 23961370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23961370/) (figures not retrieved)
- **Pakistan:** no data found.

**Middle East / West Asia**
- **Saudi Arabia, Dhahran, retrospective review, n=277 adult migraine patients:**
  - 75.8% (210/277) had fasting-related migraine during Ramadan.
  - 21.0% reported worsening during Ramadan, 6.2% improvement and 61.9% no change.
  - — [AMMS 2025, "The Impact of Fasting on Migraine in Saudi Arabia"](https://ammspub.com/index.php/amms/article/view/360)
- **Saudi Arabia, population survey:**
  - 19.6% (3,147) reported headache after breaking the Ramadan fast.
  - 84.1% of these had no prior headache diagnosis.
  - — [AlAmri et al. 2021, Cephalalgia, 10.1177/03331024211017915](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03331024211017915)
- **Saudi Arabia, Jeddah medical students/interns (n=566):** exam stress and sleep disturbance were the commonest triggers. — [PMC5432687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5432687/)
- **14-country study:** susceptibility to primary headache is associated with fasting-related headache. — [PMC13345849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13345849/) (country figures not retrieved)
- **Turkey, Mollaoğlu 2013:** stress 79%, sleep 64%, diet 44%.
- **Türkiye migraine registry 2026 (EHR data from 23 private hospitals, n=11,023):**
  - Trigger counts recorded: menstruation 731, physical activity 660, stress 515, sleep change 302, skipped meals 271, weather 210.
  - These are counts in health records and almost certainly under-recorded. They imply only about 2–7% of the cohort.
  - — [PMC13526660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13526660/)
  - Ihara 2026 adds the Lodos wind as a Türkiye-specific trigger.
- **Lebanon, university students:** — [Headache Medicine](https://headachemedicine.com.br/index.php/hm/article/view/723) (figures not retrieved)
- **Iran:** no trigger-prevalence data were retrieved.

**Africa**
- **Ethiopia, Wollo University undergraduates with migraine:**
  - Noise 60.6% (225)
  - Sunlight 56.1% (208)
  - Fatigue 55.5%
  - Too much work 55.3% (205)
  - — [PMC8579184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8579184/)
- **Nigeria, Enugu medical and nursing students:** stress was the commonest trigger (61%). — [PMC3620264](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3620264/)

### Inferences
- Suggested region multipliers for a model, on top of global priors:
  - Ramadan fasting in Muslim-majority populations: strong seasonal up-weight. 76% of Saudi migraine patients report fasting-related attacks during Ramadan.
  - Sun/heat in South Asia, Africa and China: up-weight. Sunlight is 56% in Ethiopia, the top trigger in eastern India and among the top three in Chongqing.
  - Weather/barometric pressure in Japan and East Asia: up-weight, especially for women.
  - Alcohol in the Middle East: down-weight.
- The African and some Asian data come largely from students. Students skew toward stress, sleep and noise, and these samples are not comparable with Western clinic samples.

### Gaps
- No large, harmonised, multi-country survey giving per-trigger percentages by region was found.
- No trigger percentages were retrieved for Pakistan, Iran, Taiwan, Latin America beyond Brazil, or West or East Africa beyond student samples.
- The per-country tables in Iba 2023 and Ihara 2026 were not accessible.

## Q3. How does trigger reporting differ by sex, age and migraine subtype?

### Takeaway
Women report more triggers than men (mean 4.6 vs 4.3, excluding menstruation), especially stress, bright light, skipped meals and environmental factors. Patients with aura appear to report more triggers, and different ones, than patients without aura. Children report triggers almost universally (100%; mean 7). Data on chronic vs episodic migraine are sparse.

### Cited Findings
**Sex**
- **van Casteren 2021 (NL, n=6,786):**
  - Women reported stress more than men (OR 1.47), bright light more (OR 1.29) and skipping a meal more (OR 1.24).
  - Excluding menstruation, women reported more triggers: mean 4.6 ± 2.3 vs 4.3 ± 2.3 (p<0.001), across 11 triggers asked about.
  - The authors attribute the difference to a lower attack threshold driven by sex hormones.
  - — [DOI 10.1177/0333102420974362](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102420974362)
- **Mollaoğlu 2013 (Turkey):** stress, sleep and environmental factors were significantly more important triggers in women than men. — [DOI 10.1177/1359105312446773](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1359105312446773)
- **Japan:** weather was reported by 56.1% of women vs 39.2% of men; women also reported light and noise more. — [PMC13403815](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13403815/)
- **Kelman 2007:** reports sex differences (direction not retrieved); hormones affected 65.1% of women. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x)

**Aura**
- **Iliopoulos 2015 (Greece):**
  - Patients with migraine with aura (MA) reported the highest mean number of triggers per person and the highest frequency in almost all trigger categories.
  - MA patients were more likely to report oversleeping, the premenstrual period, stressful life events and hot/cold weather.
  - — [BMC Res Notes, DOI 10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7)
- **Mollaoğlu 2013:** sleep and stress were significant triggers in MA; environmental factors in migraine without aura (MO). — [DOI 10.1177/1359105312446773](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1359105312446773)
- **Hauge 2011 (MA only):** hormones 62%, light 47% and stress 42% cause at least half of attacks; alcohol 0%. — [DOI 10.1177/0333102410382795](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102410382795)
- **Kelman 2007:** reports aura vs no-aura and episodic vs chronic differences (direction not retrieved). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x)

**Age (paediatric)**
- **Neut et al. 2012, J Headache Pain, n=102 children and adolescents (France):**
  - 100% reported at least one trigger; the mean was 7 triggers per child.
  - Stress 75.5%
  - Lack of sleep 69.6%
  - Warm climate 68.6%
  - Video games 64.7%
  - Stress was the trigger most often "always" associated with attacks (24.5%).
  - — [DOI 10.1007/s10194-011-0397-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-011-0397-2)
- **Another single-centre paediatric study (Korea, Clin Exp Pediatr 2012):**
  - Sleep disturbance 76.5%
  - Academic stress 66.7%
  - Motion sickness 62.7%
  - Fatigue 52.9%
  - — [e-cep.org](https://e-cep.org/journal/view.php?number=20125555745)
- **Other paediatric data:** a French prospective child cohort ([Solotareff 2017, J Child Neurol, 10.1177/0883073817705251](https://doi.org/10.1177/0883073817705251)) and a 2026 study of patient- vs parent-reported triggers ([Zahran 2026, DMCN, 10.1111/dmcn.70375](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dmcn.70375)) exist. Their figures were not retrieved.

**Chronic vs episodic**
- The only direct evidence found is Kelman 2007, which reports differences without a retrievable direction.
- A 2025 review on environmental triggers and chronification exists. — [PubMed 41426981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41426981/) (not retrieved)

### Inferences
- A sex term is justified. Give women a higher baseline sensitivity to stress, light, fasting and weather, plus a menstrual term.
- Paediatric users need a different trigger set. Screens/video games, heat and sleep loss are prominent, and children endorse many triggers.
- Aura status plausibly raises the number of reported triggers, but the evidence comes from small Greek and Turkish samples.

### Gaps
- No quantified chronic vs episodic comparison of per-trigger rates was retrievable.
- Kelman's subgroup directions could not be confirmed.
- No adult age-gradient data (for example, older adults vs young adults) were found.

## Q4. How many triggers does a typical patient report?

### Takeaway
Adults in clinics typically endorse about 4–5 triggers, and more than 80% report more than one. Children endorse about 7. Prospective N-of-1 analysis also finds about 4 factors per patient that are statistically associated with attacks.

### Cited Findings
- **Netherlands, adults (n=6,786), 11-item list:** mean 4.6 triggers in women and 4.3 in men, excluding menstruation. — [van Casteren 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102420974362)
- **US clinic (n=200):** 91% reported at least 1 trigger and 82.5% reported several. — [Andress-Rothrock 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21044280/)
- **US clinic (n=1,207):** 75.9% reported any trigger. — [Kelman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x)
- **Meta-analysis:** 81% report at least one trigger; the count of triggers endorsed rises with checklist length (OR 1.18 per category). — [Pellegrino 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102417727535)
- **Children (n=102):** mean 7 triggers per child; 100% reported at least one. — [Neut 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-011-0397-2)
- **Prospective N-of-1 (n=326):** mean 4 factors associated with attacks per patient; 87% had at least one; 85% of profiles were unique. — [Peris 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27179352/)
- **Greece:** migraine with aura had the highest mean number of triggers per person. — [Iliopoulos 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7)

### Inferences
- For onboarding, expect users to select about 4–5 triggers from a checklist of about 10–15 items. Longer checklists will inflate selections.
- Self-report and prospective analysis agree on about 4 factors per patient. However, Peris 2017 shows that the specific factors often differ from what the patient believes, so priors should be updated with diary data.

### Gaps
- The mean number of triggers in Kelman 2007 and Pellegrino 2018 was not retrieved.
- No regional comparison of trigger counts was found.
