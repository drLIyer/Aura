# Falling estrogen and fasting outrank blamed triggers

Worldwide, people with migraine name **stress (about 58% pooled, 64–80% in clinic series), sleep disturbance (about 41% pooled), menstruation (about 65% of women), fasting or skipped meals (about 46–57%) and weather (about 47–53%)** as their main triggers. Prospective diaries tell a narrower story. The effects that hold up are **perimenstrual estrogen withdrawal (about 1.7–2× risk), prolonged fasting (about 1.3–2.5× migraine days), stress "let-down" (OR 1.5–1.9 in one small study) and poor sleep efficiency (OR 1.39)**. Ordinary alcohol intake, short sleep, bright light and exercise come out near null, and weather effects are about 5–8% per 5°C or 5 mmHg at the population level. Migraine affects about **1.1–1.2 billion people, an age-standardized prevalence of about 14%**. GBD 2023 puts it at **17.6% in females and 10.5% in males**, and migraine is the leading cause of disability burden among young women. The sex gap opens at puberty, peaks at about 3:1 around age 40, and tracks estrogen across menarche, pregnancy, postpartum and menopause. In the US, prevalence rises as income falls, and American Indian/Alaska Native adults have the highest rates. The most consistent racial disparity lies in **diagnosis and access to care, not in prevalence**: Black and Hispanic patients are diagnosed less often, at every age. For a personal risk model, these findings argue for strong priors on a few hormonal and metabolic triggers, weak priors on most self-reported ones, and room for each person's own profile, since 85% of prospectively derived trigger profiles are unique.

## Stress leads every self-report list, but checklists inflate the numbers

About three-quarters to nine-tenths of clinic patients name at least one trigger. In a US tertiary clinic of 1,207 patients, **75.9% reported any trigger**. The leading ones were **stress 79.7%, hormones 65.1% (women only), missed meals or fasting 57.3%, weather 53.2%, sleep disturbance 49.8%, odours 43.7%, neck pain 38.4%, lights 38.1%, alcohol 37.8%, smoke 35.7%, food 26.9%, exercise 22.1% and sexual activity 5.2%** ([Kelman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01303.x)). A meta-analysis of 85 studies (27,122 participants, migraine and tension-type headache combined) found **81% (95% CI 75–86%)** endorsed a trigger. Pooled endorsement was **0.58 for stress and 0.41 for sleep**. Endorsement rose with every extra trigger category on the questionnaire (**OR 1.18 per category**), which shows how much the measurement method shapes the result ([Pellegrino 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102417727535)). A pooled analysis of weather gives an endorsement of about **47% (0.40–0.54)** ([J Neurol 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13078-0)). Caffeine sat near the bottom, at about 8% in one US series ([Andress-Rothrock 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21044280/)); that figure was relayed through a citing paper.

Typical patients pick about four or five triggers. Dutch adults chose a mean of **4.6 (women) vs 4.3 (men)** from an 11-item list, excluding menstruation. Women reported stress (OR 1.47), bright light (OR 1.29) and skipped meals (OR 1.24) more often than men ([van Casteren 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102420974362)). Children endorse even more. In a French paediatric sample, **100% reported a trigger, with a mean of seven**: stress 75.5%, lack of sleep 69.6%, warm climate 68.6% and video games 64.7% ([Neut 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-011-0397-2)). Patients with aura appear to report more triggers per person ([Iliopoulos 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13104-015-1390-7)), but the evidence comes from small samples.

Two caveats matter for interpreting these numbers. First, several "triggers" are probably early symptoms of the attack itself. Light sensitivity, neck pain, food cravings and fatigue are classic premonitory symptoms ([J Neurol 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-020-10344-1)). Second, when patients kept 90-day diaries covering 33 factors, each person had about **four factors actually associated with attacks, 85% of the profiles were unique**, and only 8 of the 33 factors were significant across the pooled population ([Peris 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27179352/)). Self-report and diaries agree on how many triggers people have, but often disagree on which ones they are.

## Regional differences sit in the second tier: Ramadan, barometric pressure and sun

Stress and sleep top the lists in every region surveyed. A review of 42 studies from 13 Asian countries found **fatigue and weather prominent in East Asia, fasting in West Asia, and food and alcohol triggers shaped by culture** ([Iba 2023](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1169795/full)). A 2026 expert review from 11 countries confirms stress, menstruation and sleep as the global leaders. It also lists local triggers: fasting during Ramadan or Yom Kippur, barometric pressure in East Asia, the Lodos wind in Türkiye, the midnight sun in the Arctic and watermelon in Brazil ([Ihara 2026](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/a-global-perspective-on-migraine-attack-triggers-understanding-di/)).

The regional figures that exist are informative but hard to compare. In a Saudi clinic review, **75.8% of 277 migraine patients had fasting-related attacks during Ramadan** ([AMMS 2025](https://ammspub.com/index.php/amms/article/view/360)). In Japan, weather change was reported by **56.1% of women vs 39.2% of men** ([PMC13403815](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13403815/)), although a 2025 review calls the weather effect in Japan "unclear" ([Front Neurol 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2025.1600822/full)). In Chongqing, China, sleep disturbance, negative affect and sunlight led the list, and weather change separated migraine from tension-type headache (**OR 3.1, 1.9–4.8**) ([PubMed 23356519](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356519/)). Sun exposure was the top trigger in an eastern Indian population survey ([Neurol India](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/neur/fulltext/10.4103/0028-3886.217979~prevalence-burden-and-risk-factors-of-migraine-a)). Indian clinic patients reported **stress 70%, exhaustion or travel 52.5%, fasting 46.3% and sleep deprivation 44.4%** ([Yadav 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1526-4637.2009.00725.x)). Ethiopian students with migraine named **noise 60.6% and sunlight 56.1%** ([PMC8579184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8579184/)). Stress led at 64% in Brazil ([Fukui 2008](https://www.scielo.br/j/anp/a/8dGbJR6MJRfR9pvdxNBgFVs/abstract/?lang=en)) and at 79% in Turkey ([Mollaoğlu 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1359105312446773)).

Taken together, a model can reasonably use seasonal up-weights for fasting in Muslim-majority populations, sun and heat in South Asia, Africa and China, and pressure change in East Asia (especially for women), and a down-weight for alcohol in the Middle East. No harmonised multi-country survey gives per-trigger percentages by region, and much of the African and Asian data comes from students, who skew toward stress, sleep and noise.

## Prospective diaries shrink most triggers to modest effects

The prospective evidence ranks triggers quite differently from self-report. **Menstruation is the best-supported trigger.** In 155 women followed with diaries, migraine was **1.7× more likely in the two days before menses** and more than twice as likely on day 1 and in the first days of bleeding ([MacGregor 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15277635/); [AAN](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/home/PressRelease/219)). Urinary hormone assays tie these attacks to **falling estrogen** ([MacGregor 2006](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.wnl.0000233888.18228.19)). **Fasting** reliably provokes headache. On Yom Kippur, **39% of fasters vs 7% of non-fasters** had headache, though mostly of a tension-type character ([Mosek & Korczyn 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7501139/)). During Ramadan, migraine days rose from **3.7 to 9.4 per month** in one small crossover study ([Abu-Salameh 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-010-0242-z)), and attack frequency rose about 1.3× in 292 Egyptian patients ([PubMed 34726767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34726767/)).

For **stress**, the clearest signal comes from a decline in stress, the "let-down" effect. A drop in perceived stress predicted attacks over the next 6–18 h (**OR 1.5–1.9**), whereas the stress level itself mostly did not ([Lipton 2014](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/200156)). That study had only 17 completers. Higher stress did predict next-day migraine in a larger 178-person diary cohort ([Holsteen 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/)). For **sleep**, low self-reported sleep efficiency predicted next-day headache (**OR 1.39, 1.06–1.81**), but **short sleep duration did not**, and sleeping more than 8.5 h was linked to *lower* risk two days later ([Bertisch 2020](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000008740); [summary](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/how-does-sleep-affect-migraine-risk)).

**Alcohol**, one of the most cited dietary triggers, is null at ordinary intake. In 487 app users, the next-day OR was **1.01 (0.91–1.11)**, and drinking two days earlier was linked to *lower* risk ([Vives-Mestres 2022](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.14428)). Only **5 or more drinks** showed a signal, OR 2.08 (1.16–3.73), and the outcome there was headache rather than migraine ([Mostofsky 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07853890.2020.1758340)). **Caffeine** follows a nonlinear pattern (p=0.024). One to two servings gave OR 0.84, and 5 or more gave OR 2.61 (0.97–7.05) ([Mostofsky 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002934319302104)). A *decrease* in caffeine predicted attacks, which is consistent with withdrawal ([Holsteen 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/)). **Weather** effects are real but small. In a case-crossover study of 7,054 emergency visits, each 5°C rise gave **OR 1.075 (1.021–1.133)**, and lower pressure raised the risk only of non-migraine headache ([Mukamal 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19273827/)). A 12-month diary study found no pooled weather effect, only a weather-sensitive subgroup ([Hoffmann 2015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acn3.139)). A 2025 meta-analysis reportedly gives pooled ORs of 1.15 for temperature and 1.07 for pressure, but these could not be verified against the paper ([J Neurol 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13078-0)). Provocation testing undercuts **light and exercise**. Among 27 patients with aura who believed light or exercise triggered their attacks, **none developed an attack after photostimulation, and only 4 of 12 did after exercise** ([Hougaard 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3620071/)).

Premonitory symptoms predict attacks better than most triggers do. **72% of recorded premonitory symptoms were followed by migraine**, mostly within about 6 h ([Giffin 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12654956/)). This sets a realistic ceiling for prediction. Trigger-only models reach a validated **AUC of about 0.56–0.65** ([Houle 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28699328/); [Holsteen 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33022773/)), and a wearable model reached 0.62 ([Stubberud 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37096352/)). A company-affiliated app study reported AUC 0.89 in 53,065 users, likely helped by recent-attack features ([Rabany 2026](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WN9.0000000000000144)).

### Trigger priors for a personal risk model

Priors refer to attack onset in the next 24–48 h relative to the person's own baseline. "Conservative" means shrunk toward 1.0 to allow for small samples, reverse causation from the prodrome, and trigger-prone clinic samples. Each personal model should include a per-person random effect, so that individual diary data can move these priors.

## Women carry most of the burden, and estrogen explains when

The headline figures are consistent across sources. GBD 2019 gives a global age-standardized prevalence of **14,107 per 100,000**, highest in Belgium, Italy and Germany ([Safiri 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34001771/)). GBD 2021 gives about 1.16–1.2 billion cases ([PMC12629824](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12629824/)), with the highest regional rates in **Latin America and the Caribbean (18,595 per 100,000) and Western Europe (18,171)** ([Pain Ther 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40122-024-00690-7)). GBD 2023 gives **14.1% overall, 17.6% in females and 10.5% in males**, with headache YLD rates of **739.9 per 100,000 in women vs 346.1 in men** ([Lancet Neurol 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(25)00402-8/fulltext)). A pooled survey analysis independent of GBD also lands at **14.0%** ([Stovner 2022](https://doaj.org/article/ffe6ba39997a4ba198e232d3ef714a87)). Migraine is the second-leading cause of YLDs worldwide. In females aged 15–49, headache disorders carry a DALY rate of **1,016 per 100,000, second only to gynaecological disease and ahead of depression** ([Steiner 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7708887/)). Absolute cases in women of childbearing age rose **48% since 1990, to about 494 million**, while age-standardized rates stayed flat; population growth, not more migraine per person, explains the rise ([J Headache Pain 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10194-024-01798-z)).

The modelled GBD female:male ratio of about 1.7 understates the gap seen in surveys, because age-standardizing across all ages includes children and older adults, in whom the gap is small. US surveys give **17.3% of women vs 5.7% of men** in AMPP ([Consultant360](https://www.consultant360.com/exclusive/neurology/ahs/women-are-twice-likely-have-migraine)) and **17.5% vs 8.6%** in NHIS 2003 ([Victor 2010 summary](https://s4me.info/threads/migraine-prevalence-by-age-and-sex-in-the-united-states-a-life-span-study-2010-victor-et-al.49837/)). The gap has a clear age course. Before about age 12, boys are equally or more often affected. After puberty the ratio rises from **about 2:1 at age 20 to 3.3:1 at age 40** ([Frontiers Neurol 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2024.1441129/full)). Childhood and adolescent prevalence pools at **7.7–11%** ([Abu-Arafeh 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20875042/); [Onofri 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36782182/)). In GBD 2021, incidence peaks at ages 10–14 and prevalence peaks at **40–44 in women (27.5%)** and earlier, at 30–34, in men ([PMC13529543](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13529543/)). Prevalence declines after about 50.

Each hormonal life stage follows a "falling estrogen triggers, stable estrogen protects" pattern. Later menarche lowers risk, while oral contraception and parity raise it ([PubMed 38215242](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38215242/)). In a prospective Italian cohort of 47 women with migraine without aura, migraine improved in **83% by the second trimester and 87% by the third**, but returned within a month of delivery in **55%** ([Sances 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12662187)). Migraine with aura improves less often in pregnancy ([Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/diseases-diagnoses/headache-pain/migraine-during-pregnancy/31515/)). Perimenopause raises the odds of high-frequency headache by **60%** ([AMPP via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/01/160121130700.htm)). Natural menopause improved migraine in 67% of women, versus 33% after surgical menopause, which worsened it in 67% ([Ripa 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4548761/)). Current hormone therapy is associated with higher migraine risk (**RR 1.49**) ([Neurology Advisor](https://www.neurologyadvisor.com/news/estrogen-use-migraine-incidence-acog/)). Gender-minority data are sparse but point the same way. In one small Dutch study, trans women on estrogen had a migraine prevalence of **26%, against 25% in cis women and 7.5% in cis men** ([Pringsheim & Gooren](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8405888_Migraine_prevalence_in_male_to_female_transsexuals_on_hormone_therapy)). Newer cohorts find no difference between estrogen and testosterone users ([Zavala 2026](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.15076)).

## Race and income shape diagnosis more than they shape disease

US racial comparisons depend on how migraine is measured. On the NHIS "severe headache or migraine" item, **American Indian/Alaska Native adults are highest (17.7–18.4%)**, White, Black and Hispanic adults cluster around **14.5–16%**, and Asian Americans are lowest (**9.2–11.3%**) ([Loder 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644596); [Burch 2018](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.13281)). Surveys using formal diagnostic criteria instead find lower prevalence in Black than in White Americans. AMPP reported **13.7% vs 17.3% in women** ([Lipton 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17261680/)), and a 1996 Baltimore County study reported a similar ordering, with Asian Americans lowest ([Stewart 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8710124/)). The low Asian American figures match lower survey prevalence in Asia itself: **Japan 8.4%, China 9.3% and Taiwan 9.1%** ([Sakai 1997](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-2982.1997.1701015.x); [Yu 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22590713/); [Wang 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1046/j.1468-2982.2000.00085.x)). Some low- and middle-income settings report high rates; a Karnataka survey found **25.2%** ([Kulkarni 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26197976/)). GBD 2023 places the highest migraine YLD rate in North Africa and the Middle East and the lowest in sub-Saharan Africa ([Lancet Neurol 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(25)00402-8/fulltext)). African population data remain sparse ([PMC10996250](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10996250/)). Genetic studies are no help in explaining ancestry differences. A multi-ancestry Million Veteran Program GWAS found 49 loci ([Gasperi 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12863721/)), but African-ancestry samples yielded few hits, which mostly reflects their small size.

The socioeconomic gradient is clearer. In AMPP, prevalence rose as household income fell, in both sexes, independent of race. Low-income groups had **higher incidence but the same remission rate**, which points to social causation rather than disabled people drifting into poverty ([Stewart 2013](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e3182a43b32)). In NHIS 2015, prevalence was **21.4% among unemployed adults and 19.9% below $35,000 family income, against 15.3% overall** ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/study-summary-a-targeted-systematic-review-to-determine-the-prevalence-of-migraine-and-severe-headache-in-the-united-states)).

Disparities are sharpest at diagnosis. In 49 US children's hospitals, emergency staff diagnosed migraine in **45.5% of White, 28.2% of Black and 28.3% of Hispanic children** who presented with headache. Black children were also less likely to get an MRI (aOR 0.56) ([Kellier 2025](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000213351)). Ambulatory visits coded as migraine ran at **176 per 10,000 for White, 133 for Black and 90 for Hispanic people**, while visits for nonspecific headache were similar across groups ([Loder 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644596)). Among adults with disabling migraine, African Americans had received a diagnosis less often than White patients (**47% vs 70%**) ([Nicholson 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2443411/)). This was a small single-site study, and it is the likely origin of the widely repeated "14% vs 37% acute prescription" figure ([AMF](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/racial-disparities-in-migraine-care/)). Treatment once patients are diagnosed is more mixed. National 2006–2013 visit data showed **no significant racial difference in triptan or DHE prescribing (15.3% Black, 19.4% White, 17.7% Hispanic)** ([Charleston & Burke 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0333102417716933)). Newer data show gaps in preventive use (**20% White vs 7% Black vs 10% Hispanic**, in small subgroups) ([PubMed 40767929](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40767929/)) and in CGRP-drug prescribing ([Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/racial-gender-inequities-cgrp-gepant-prescriptions-2024a1000blc)). White participants averaged **about 83%** of migraine trial enrollees ([Robbins 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28127754/)).

## Conclusion

The triggers people blame most, such as stress, light, alcohol and foods, are mostly weak or confounded by the prodrome. The ones with solid prospective support are physiological transitions: falling estrogen, prolonged fasting, a drop in stress and a drop in caffeine. A personal model therefore gains more from tracking *changes* (cycle day, hours since eating, day-over-day stress and caffeine deltas) than from tracking exposure levels, and should treat photophobia, neck pain and fatigue as short-horizon warning signs rather than causes. Even well-built trigger models plateau around AUC 0.6–0.7, so honest uncertainty, individual random effects and learning from each user's own diary matter more than a long checklist.

On who is affected, estrogen dynamics explain most of the sex gap and its life-course shape, and income gradients point to social causes. Measurement is the weak link for race and ethnicity. Prevalence estimates built on diagnoses, claims records or self-applied labels will under-count Black and Hispanic people with migraine, because those groups are diagnosed less often. Any model or dataset that uses "diagnosed migraine" as ground truth will inherit that bias unless it screens for migraine with symptom criteria directly.
